Where we started

A white logo on a light gray wall

Your logo is white. The top part of the wall on West Cary Street is painted light gray. White and light gray are almost the same brightness. When two colors are this close, the eye cannot separate them from far away.

Here is your own logo file. On the left it sits on the real color of your wall. On the right it sits on Haru green. Same logo, same size, both sides:

How sign painters measure this

We use a number to score how easy letters are to see. A higher number is easier. Black letters on a white wall score about 21. That is the best score you can get.

White letters on this gray wall score about 1.3. That is almost invisible.

Painting more carefully cannot fix this. The problem is the two colors, not the brushwork.

How we fixed it

A green box behind the white letters

We did not want to change your logo. The letters stay white, exactly as your designer made them. Instead, we paint a box of Haru green behind them.

The green box is easy to see from far away. The white letters show up clearly against the green.

I want to be honest with you. White on green is still not the strongest choice. But it keeps your brand colors, people can find the sign from far away, and the letters will be large. It is a very big improvement over invisible.

Another idea we tested

About drawing a line around the letters

When I showed your mom the problem with white on light gray, she asked a good question: could we paint a very thin dark line around the outside of the white letters, so the logo could sit right on the gray wall with no box behind it?

That is a real sign painting method, so it was worth testing properly. Here is what we found.

1. A line only fixes the edge

The line goes around the outside of each letter. The inside of the letter is still white, and the wall behind it is still light gray. From across the street, you would see a thin dark shape, but the letter itself would still blend into the wall. The green box fixes the whole letter, not just its edge.

2. A thin line gets very hard to see from far away

People will read this sign from about 50 feet away. At that distance a quarter inch line is right at the edge of what the eye can pick out. It reads as a faint smudge more than a line.

To read clearly as a line from 50 feet, it needs to be roughly three quarters of an inch thick. At that thickness it is not a thin outline any more. It becomes a bold black edge, and it changes the look of your logo.

3. It costs a lot more

Painting a clean line around every curve of every letter, by hand, is slow and difficult work. It would add many hours to the job. That is a large cost to fix a problem the green box already fixes.

So the line is possible, but it works less well than the green box and it costs much more. It was still worth asking about, and testing it is how we knew the green box was the right answer. If you would like to see the line version as a picture anyway, just ask and I will make one. This is your family's building.

Your biggest question

Why the small words got their own sign

You asked why we moved "Curated Japanese & Korean Market" away from the Haru name. This was not a style choice. It is about how far away people will be standing.

The rule sign painters use

A letter needs about 1 inch of height for every 10 feet that a person stands away from it.

This sign will be about 15 feet up in the air. People read it from 40 to 60 feet away, from the other side of the street and from the crosswalks. So the letters need to be 5 to 6 inches tall.

If we kept the small words inside the big logo, those letters would only be about 2 to 3 inches tall. From across the street they would look like a gray blur above the name. Nobody could read them, and drivers definitely could not.

So we moved those words to their own sign, on the corner where the two streets meet. There we can make the letters large enough to actually read. Everyone walking up Cary Street or Belmont Avenue will see it.

The part after the drawings

How the painting actually happens

These are hand-painted signs, not stickers or printed panels. Here is the real plan, so you know what the work looks like and how long it takes.

About 5 to 6 working days of painting

1 day to prepare. I make a full size pattern of every letter before I touch the wall.

3 to 4 hours to transfer the design onto the building, so the letters land in exactly the right place at the right size.

3 days for the three main signs. 1 day for the 3030 and the Japanese and Korean writing.

The transfer happens late at night, around 2:30am to 6am on a weekday. Cary Street is busy, and doing it at night keeps the sidewalk clear for your neighbors and their customers, and keeps my ladder out of everyone's way.

I am not renting a lift. A friend in the sign business is lending me a bucket truck for the few hours of transfer work, and the rest is painted from my own ladder. That keeps the equipment cost off your bill.

Opening day is August 20. Those painting days have to fit somewhere before then, and they can only start once the design is approved and the job is on my calendar.
